The machine

Motorcycle overview

A 293 cm³ fuel-injected two-stroke built for terrain that stops other bikes. Everything in this handbook assumes that bike, that engine, and a rider who would rather fix it at the trailhead than trailer it home.

How to read this handbook

Every figure carries its origin. Manual means the value is transcribed from KTM's Owner's Manual 2026 for the 250/300 EXC family, item no. 3240239en. Workshop marks practical guidance written for this handbook: estimated times, difficulty ratings, common mistakes and setup advice. Dealer marks work KTM restricts to an authorised workshop.

Where the source manual gives one value for standard models and another for special models, both appear. The manual does not state which bucket HardEnduro falls into for those fasteners, so this handbook shows both rather than picking one for you. Check your VIN-specific documentation before torquing a triple clamp.

What this bike is

The 300 EXC HardEnduro is the extreme-terrain variant of KTM's 300 EXC. It shares the 293.15 cm³ single-cylinder two-stroke, the six-speed gearbox and the hydraulic clutch with the rest of the family. What changes is the suspension calibration, the fork part number, and a handful of components chosen for slow, technical, low-traction riding rather than fast enduro loops.

Engine

293 cm³ two-stroke

Liquid-cooled single with diaphragm intake and an electronically actuated exhaust control flap. Fuel injected, with a separate oil tank and metering pump: no premixing.

Chassis

Chromoly central tube frame

300 mm front and 310 mm rear travel, WP XPLOR CC fork and a linkless WP PDS shock. Around 106 kg without fuel.

Service rhythm

Counted in hours, not miles

The whole schedule runs on engine operating hours. The instrument tracks them. A hard enduro hour is worth several trail hours in wear terms.

No premix: how lubrication works

This is the detail that catches people moving from an older two-stroke. There is a separate oil tank holding roughly 0.8 l of fully synthetic two-stroke oil, and an electronically controlled pump meters it into the engine according to load and revs. You fill the fuel tank with plain unleaded and the oil tank separately.

Warning · Engine damage

Do not add two-stroke oil to the fuel tank. The engine is already receiving metered oil, and premixing on top of that fouls plugs, clogs the exhaust control and loads the silencer packing. Equally, never run the bike with the oil tank empty or the oil warning active: the lubrication is not passive, and there is no reserve.

Check spoke tension after the first half hour of operation on a new bike or a freshly laced wheel. New spokes bed into the nipples and the whole wheel loses tension at once. Six newton metres per nipple, worked around the wheel in sequence.

What this handbook does not cover

This is a maintenance and setup handbook, not a rebuild manual. Work that requires splitting cases, replacing the connecting rod or crankshaft pin, or servicing the fork and shock internals needs KTM's Repair Manual, special tools and specifications that are not published in the owner's documentation. Those jobs are marked Dealer throughout. Attempting them from this document alone will cost you an engine.

The exhaust control flap

The power valve is not a passive spring-and-ball-ramp mechanism on this engine: it is driven by an electric actuator under ECU control, which is why the fault code list has nine separate entries for it. The Z-dimension in the table above is the flap height specification checked during minor engine service.

Caution · Carbon is the enemy

Every fastener in the exhaust control assembly is an M5 running between 5 and 8 Nm, and almost all of them take Loctite 243. They are small, they sit in an oily carbon-laden environment, and they strip readily. Use a quality driver, use the correct torque, and never guess it by feel: 8 Nm on an M5 is much less than your wrist thinks it is.

Service life expectations

The schedule sets minor engine service at 90 operating hours and major service, including engine removal, at 24 months. Manual There is also a note in the manual that under certain conditions the piston should be changed every 10 operating hours.

Warning · Competition use shortens everything

The 10-hour piston interval in the source manual applies to specific high-load use. If you race the bike, the published 90-hour figure is not your interval. Judge by leak-down, ring end gap and how the bike starts hot, and consult your dealer about what applies to your riding. Guessing wrong here means a holed piston in the middle of nowhere.

Tip · Gearing for extreme terrain

Going up two teeth at the rear from 50 to 52 shortens first gear by about 4 % and makes the bike easier to ride at walking pace, at the cost of top speed you were not using on a hill climb anyway. Going down at the front has a much larger effect per tooth and tightens the chain arc around the countershaft, which increases wear. Prefer changing the rear sprocket. Whichever you change, re-check chain tension and the chain guide afterwards. Workshop

The primary gear nut (M18 LH×1.5, 150 Nm) and the crankshaft nut (M12 LH×1, 60 Nm) are both left-hand thread. Turning them the familiar way tightens them, and at those torques you will shear or gall something before you realise. Confirm thread direction before applying any force.

Water in the gear oil

If gear oil comes out milky, the water pump seal or its radial shaft seal ring has failed and coolant is entering the transmission. This is a common failure on hard-worked two-strokes and it is not something to ride out: water in the gearbox destroys bearings quickly. Stop, drain, and replace the seal before the next ride. Warning · Compressed nitrogen

The shock body contains nitrogen at 10 bar. Parts move unpredictably if it is detached incorrectly, and the reservoir must never be opened, drilled or heated. Internal shock service is Dealer work. External clicker adjustment, covered in chapter 9, is safe and encouraged.

The baseline this bike is set up for

KTM delivers offroad models set for a standard rider weight of 75 to 85 kg with full protective clothing. If you are outside that range, clickers will not fix it: small differences can be compensated with preload, large differences need different springs. Fitting the correct spring rate is the single highest-value suspension change most riders can make, and it costs less than a set of clickers' worth of frustration.

Suspension tuning guide

Set the shock first, then the fork. That order is not arbitrary: the shock determines the bike's attitude, and the fork responds to whatever attitude you leave it in.

The order of operations

  1. Confirm spring rates against your weight in full kit. No amount of clicker work fixes a wrong spring. Tables are in chapter 8.
  2. Set rear static sag and rider sag. Targets: 38 mm static, 110 mm rider sag.
  3. Set shock damping from the published baseline, then adjust.
  4. Set fork damping from the published baseline.
  5. Ride the same loop after each change. One change at a time.
Tip · How clicker counting works

All published click counts are measured from fully closed. Turn the adjuster clockwise until the last perceptible click, then count back anticlockwise. Do not force it past the stop: the needle and seat are soft and easy to damage. Manual

High-speed and low-speed compression

These refer to the speed at which the suspension compresses, not the speed of the bike. Low-speed compression acts on long undulations and rider inputs: weight transfer under braking, rolling over a log at walking pace. High-speed compression acts on sharp impacts: landing from a drop, square-edged rocks. The two ranges overlap, so changing one shifts the other.

Caution · The most common tuning mistake

Chasing a harsh front end with clickers when the real cause is 1.4 bar in the front tyre. Check tyre pressure, fork oil level and steering head bearing play before you change a single click. All three feel like damping problems and none of them are.

A note on hard enduro setup

Extreme terrain wants the opposite of what a fast enduro rider wants: soft, sensitive, plenty of sag, generous rebound. The Comfort column above is the sane starting point, and many riders go softer still. That comes at a genuine cost in bottoming resistance, so if your loop includes anything fast you are making a real trade. See chapter 27.

Using a torque wrench properly

Range

One wrench does not cover this bike

Published values span 0.7 Nm to 150 Nm. A click wrench is accurate in roughly the middle 80 % of its range. You need at least two, realistically three: a 0.4–5 Nm driver, a 5–25 Nm 3/8″, and a 20–100 Nm 1/2″.

Technique

Smooth, single pull

Pull steadily to the click and stop. Do not re-click to check: each additional click adds torque. Hold the handle at its centre mark, not the end.

Storage

Wind it back down

Store a click wrench at its lowest setting, never at zero and never loaded. Calibration drifts if the spring is left compressed.

Threads

Clean and dry unless told otherwise

Published torque assumes clean threads. Oiled threads reach the same clamp load at meaningfully lower torque, so an oiled fastener taken to book torque is overtightened.

Safety that applies to everything

Warning · Before any work
  • Support the bike on a proper stand on level ground. A bike that falls while you are under it will hurt you.
  • Let the engine and exhaust cool before touching either. Gear oil, coolant and exhaust all cause serious burns.
  • Never run the engine in an enclosed space. Two-stroke exhaust is toxic and it accumulates fast.

Five habits that prevent most failures

  1. Clean before you open. Grit that falls into a gearbox, a brake caliper or an airbox does more damage than the wear you were fixing.
  2. Lay fasteners out in the order you removed them. A magnetic tray with a rough map beats trying to remember which of four similar M6 screws was longer.

  4. Apply threadlocker to a clean, dry thread. On oil or old locker residue it will not cure and you have gained nothing.
  5. Do a slow lap of the bike before you ride it. More bikes are damaged by something left loose after a service than by the wear that prompted it.

Inspect brake pads and discs

10 min Difficulty Manual
Tools
  • Torch
  • Vernier caliper or micrometer
  • Steel rule
Consumables
  • Brake cleaner
  • Lint-free cloth
Replacement parts
  • None unless a limit is reached
Warning · Worn pads reduce braking

Pads below the minimum thickness must be replaced immediately. Running past the friction material scores the disc and can destroy it in a single ride.

Steps

  1. Put the bike on a stand so both wheels can be turned freely.
  2. Shine a torch into each caliper from behind and check the friction material on both pads. The outer pad often wears faster on a floating caliper.
  3. Measure the remaining friction material. Minimum is 1 mm, measured on the friction material only, not including the backing plate.
  4. Check both pads for cracks, glazing, contamination and chunks missing from the edges.
  5. Confirm the retaining pin is present and secured, and that the pads sit square in the carrier.
  6. Measure disc thickness at several points around the disc, especially inside the worn band. Front limit 2.5 mm, rear limit 3.5 mm on standard models and 3.7 mm on special models.
  7. Spin each wheel and watch the disc through the caliper for run-out or a bent section.
  8. Check the brake lines along their whole length for chafing, cracking and correct routing, and check the fittings for weeping.

Common mistakes

  • Measuring only the pad you can see easily and missing a worn inner pad.
  • Including the steel backing plate in the thickness measurement, which makes a worn-out pad look legal.
  • Measuring disc thickness on the unworn outer edge rather than in the swept band.
  • Cleaning pads with a petroleum-based degreaser, which contaminates the friction material permanently.

Replace brake pads

30 min per end Difficulty Workshop
Tools
  • Allen and Torx keys per fastener
  • Torque wrench, 5–25 Nm
  • Piston retraction tool or wide flat blade
  • Small pick for the retaining pin clip
Consumables

Warning · Unapproved pads change braking behaviour

Pad compound determines bite, fade resistance and how the brake behaves wet. Fitting pads not approved for this bike changes the braking action in ways you will discover at the worst moment. Brake fluid is also harmful: keep it off skin, eyes and clothing, wear gloves and eye protection, and seek medical advice on contact.

Steps

  1. Clean the caliper and the surrounding area thoroughly before opening anything.
  2. Check the fluid level in the reservoir first. Pushing the pistons back will raise it, and an overfull reservoir will overflow onto paint and plastic.
  3. Remove the retaining pin clip, then withdraw the pin and lift out the old pads.
  4. Inspect the caliper: pistons free of corrosion, dust seals intact, no fluid weeping.
  5. Clean the caliper body and carrier with brake cleaner. Do not use a wire brush on piston faces or seals.
  6. Retract the pistons slowly and squarely. If a piston is stiff, stop: a seized caliper needs rebuilding, not forcing.
  7. Fit the new pads with any anti-rattle spring in its original orientation, then insert the pin and secure the clip.
  8. If the caliper was removed, refit and torque it.

    Front caliper screw25 NmM8 · Loctite 243 Caliper on bracket45 NmM10 · Loctite 243

  9. Pump the lever or pedal until it goes firm. Do this before the bike moves, not after.
  10. Re-check the fluid level and top up to the mark with fresh DOT 5.1 if needed.
  11. Bed the new pads in with a series of progressively harder stops from low speed on a safe surface.

Threadlocker

Caliper mounting screws take Loctite 243, applied to clean dry threads. Brake disc screws also take 243 at 14 Nm if the disc is disturbed.

Common mistakes

  • Riding off without pumping the lever, so the first application at the first corner does nothing.
  • Forcing a seized piston back and tearing the dust seal, which then admits water and destroys the caliper.
  • Reservoir overflow onto plastics: DOT 5.1 strips paint quickly.
  • Getting brake cleaner or fingerprints on the new friction surface.
  • Refitting a caliper screw without threadlocker because the old one still looked coated.

Change brake and clutch fluid

45 min per circuit Difficulty Manual

Tools
  • Ring spanner for the bleed nipple
  • Clear bleed hose and catch bottle
  • Vacuum or pressure bleeder (optional but faster)
  • Screwdriver for reservoir cover
Consumables
  • Fresh sealed DOT 5.1
  • Brake cleaner, lint-free cloth
  • Gloves and eye protection
Replacement parts
  • Bleed nipple dust caps if perished
  • Reservoir diaphragm if deformed
Warning · Brake fluid is a health hazard

Keep it away from children. Wear protective clothing and safety glasses. Do not allow contact with skin, eyes or clothing, and consult a doctor immediately if it enters the eyes. Use only fresh fluid from a sealed container: DOT 5.1 absorbs water from the air, and wet fluid boils under hard braking exactly when you need it most.

Steps

  1. Position the reservoir horizontally. Clean around the cover before opening it.
  2. Remove the cover and diaphragm. Cover surrounding plastics and paint with a cloth.
  3. Fit the bleed hose to the nipple and run it into a catch bottle with a little fluid in the bottom.
  4. Open the nipple a quarter turn, apply the lever or pedal, close the nipple, release. Repeat, keeping the reservoir topped up throughout.
  5. Continue until clean fluid of the new colour runs through with no bubbles. Never let the reservoir run dry: air in the master cylinder means starting again.
  6. Close the nipple, remove the hose, refit the dust cap.
  7. Set the level to the mark, refit the diaphragm and cover without overtightening.
  8. Clean every trace of spilled fluid with water, then dry.
  9. Check lever feel and free travel. The front hand brake lever needs at least 3 mm free travel.
Tip · When the lever will not firm up

Air trapped at the highest point of the master cylinder is the usual culprit. Rotating the bars so the reservoir sits level, then tapping the lines and repeating a few slow strokes with the lever tied back overnight, will often clear what conventional bleeding cannot.

Common mistakes

  • Letting the reservoir empty mid-bleed and pulling air into the master cylinder.
  • Using fluid from a bottle opened months ago.
  • Overtightening the bleed nipple and shearing it, which turns a service into a caliper replacement.
  • Leaving spilled fluid on plastics or the frame.

Chain maintenance

Chain tension is checked at every single interval including the first hour. It is the item most likely to be wrong on a bike that has just been ridden hard.

Warning · Incorrect chain tension causes accidents

A chain that is too tight loads the countershaft bearing and can lock the rear wheel if the suspension compresses fully. A chain that is too loose can jump the sprocket, wrap the countershaft or destroy the case. Set it to specification, check it regularly.

Check chain tension

5 min Difficulty Manual
Tools
  • Steel rule or tape
  • Torch
Consumables
  • None
Replacement parts
  • None

Steps

  1. Stand the bike upright, unloaded, with the transmission in neutral.
  2. At the measuring point in the lower chain run, push the chain up until it contacts the chain guard.
  3. Measure the movement.

    Chain tension58–61 mmMeasured to chain guard

  4. Repeat at several points around the chain. Wear is never even, and the tightest spot is the one that matters.
  5. If any position falls outside the specification, adjust the chain.
Caution · Always set to the tightest spot

Rotate the wheel through a full revolution and find the tightest position, then set tension there. Setting it at a loose spot leaves the chain over-tight everywhere else.

Common mistakes

  • Measuring with the bike on a stand and the rear wheel hanging, which gives a false reading.
  • Measuring at one point only.
  • Measuring total up-and-down movement rather than movement to the chain guard.

Adjust chain tension

15 min Difficulty Manual
Tools
  • Spanner for the rear axle nut, M22
  • Spanner for the adjusting screws and lock nuts
  • Torque wrench, 20–100 Nm
  • Steel rule
Consumables

  • None unless the chain is at its adjustment limit

Steps

  1. Check current tension first, so you know how far you are moving.
  2. Loosen the rear wheel spindle nut.
  3. Loosen the lock nuts on both chain adjusters.
  4. Turn both adjusting screws by the same amount, using the scale marks on the swingarm to keep the wheel square. Equal marks on both sides is the whole point of the scale.
  5. Re-check tension at the tightest point, and repeat until it sits between 58 and 61 mm.
  6. Hold the adjusters against their stops and tighten the lock nuts.
  7. Tighten the spindle nut to specification.

    Rear wheel spindle nut80 NmM22×1.5 · 59.0 lb-ft

  8. Re-check tension one final time: torquing the spindle can pull the wheel slightly.
  9. Spin the wheel and sight down the chain from behind to confirm the run is straight.
Tip · Do not trust the swingarm marks blindly

Stamped marks can be out. If the bike has ever felt like it wants to turn one way more than the other, check alignment by measuring from the swingarm pivot to the axle on both sides with a tape, or by sighting the chain run over the sprocket. Consistent readings beat trusting a stamping.

Common mistakes

  • Adjusting one side more than the other, misaligning the wheel and wearing the chain and rear tyre unevenly.
  • Forgetting to re-check tension after torquing the spindle.
  • Setting the chain too tight because it was measured with the wheel unloaded.
  • Leaving the adjuster lock nuts loose, so tension changes on the first hard drive.

Clean, lubricate and assess chain wear

20 min Difficulty Workshop
Tools

  • Rear stand so the wheel turns freely

Caution · Never use a pressure washer on the chain

High-pressure water drives grit past the O-rings and flushes out the factory grease inside the rollers, which no external lube can replace. Use a brush, cleaner and low-pressure rinse.

Steps

  1. Support the bike so the rear wheel turns freely, in neutral.
  2. Apply chain cleaner and let it work, then brush the chain on all four faces while turning the wheel.
  3. Rinse gently and dry. Water left in the rollers is worse than the mud you removed.
  4. Apply off-road chain lube to the inner face of the chain, where centrifugal force carries it outward rather than throwing it off.

  7. Inspect the sprockets. Hooked, asymmetric or sharpened teeth mean replacement.
  8. Check the chain guide and slider for wear through to the mounting.

Air filter servicing

The cheapest job on the bike and the one that saves the most expensive component. Every 15 hours by the book, every ride in reality.

Clean and re-oil the air filter

25 min plus drying Difficulty Manual
Tools
  • Tool for the seat and airbox cover fasteners
  • Torque driver, 0.4–5 Nm
  • Two bowls or sealable bags
  • Disposable gloves
Consumables
  • Foam filter cleaner
  • Foam filter oil
  • Grease for the sealing face
  • Clean rags
Replacement parts
  • Spare air filter (keep one pre-oiled)
  • Airbox cover seal if perished
Warning · A gap here destroys an engine

Any path that lets unfiltered air past the filter puts abrasive dust straight into the cylinder. A torn filter, a poorly seated cage or a missing seal will cost you a top end. Inspect the filter for tears every time, and never run the bike without one.

Steps

  1. Clean the outside of the airbox before opening it, so dirt does not fall in when you remove the cover.
  2. Remove the seat, then the airbox cover.
  3. Release the filter retainer and withdraw the filter and cage together. Note the orientation before it comes out.
  4. Wipe the inside of the airbox and the sealing flange clean with a rag. Do not blow it out with compressed air while the intake is open.
  5. Block or cover the intake while the filter is out.
  6. Separate the foam from the cage. Wash the foam in filter cleaner, squeezing rather than wringing: wringing tears foam.
  7. Rinse and squeeze until the water runs clear, then let it dry completely. Damp foam will not take oil properly.
  8. Work filter oil evenly through the dry foam until it is uniformly coloured with no dry patches and no pooling.
  9. Refit the foam to the cage, making sure the seam sits where it belongs.
  10. Apply a light film of grease to the sealing face so the filter seals against the flange.
  11. Install the filter in the correct orientation and secure the retainer.
  12. Refit the airbox cover and the seat.

    Seat installation screw8 NmM6

Tip · Run two filters

Keep a second filter cleaned, oiled and sealed in a bag. Swapping takes two minutes at the trailhead; cleaning takes an evening. It also means a filter is never rushed back into service half dry.

Common mistakes

  • Oiling a filter that is still damp, so the oil never distributes and the foam passes dust.
  • Over-oiling until it pools, which restricts airflow and can pull oil into the intake.
  • Using petrol or aggressive solvent, which breaks down the foam.
  • Skipping grease on the sealing face, leaving a dust path around the filter.
  • Blowing the airbox out with compressed air and driving dirt into the intake tract.

Final inspection

 

Note · Deep water riding

If the bike has been submerged past the airbox, do not start it. Check the filter, the airbox and the intake tract for water before turning the engine over. Hydraulic lock will bend a connecting rod, and on a two-stroke water in the crankcase also washes out the lubrication.

  8. Refit the cap and check it seals.
  9. On HardEnduro models, run the engine until the fan cuts in and confirm it turns.

Common mistakes

  • Topping up with water only, which dilutes frost and corrosion protection.
  • Checking the level on a bike leaning on the side stand.
  • Straightening fins aggressively and splitting the radiator core.

Steps

  1. Choose two reference points: one on the rear axle, one vertically above it on the subframe or a fender bolt. Mark both.
  2. Lift the rear wheel completely off the ground and measure between the points. This is the unloaded length.
  3. Lower the bike, bounce the rear and let it settle. Measure again. Unloaded length minus this figure is static sag.

    Static sag target38 mm1.50 in

  • Chasing sag with preload when the spring rate is simply wrong for the rider.

Fault-finding without a diagnostic tool

Most electrical faults on a hard-worked enduro bike are connectors and grounds, not components. Workshop Before condemning anything expensive:

Hard enduro setup guide

Everything here is a trade. Set the bike up for extreme terrain and it gets worse at everything else. That is the point, but know what you are giving up.

Provenance

This chapter is workshop guidance, not manufacturer data. Workshop Where a figure comes from the manual it is marked. Everything else is a starting point to test, not a specification to comply with.

The four things that matter most

  1. Traction. Tyre choice and pressure beat every other change combined.
  2. Control at walking pace. Gearing, clutch feel and lever position.
  3. Suspension that tracks the ground. Soft and sensitive, with enough sag to let the wheels follow terrain.
  4. Not breaking. Protection, and fasteners that stay tight through repeated crashes.

Tyres and pressure

The manual's off-road baseline is 1.0 bar front and rear. Manual Extreme riders commonly go lower for grip, and the honest framing is a spectrum of risk:

Caution · Mousse and heat

Mousse inserts are excellent for slow technical riding and poor for anything fast or sustained: they build heat and break down. If your loop includes long fire-road transits, a mousse is the wrong choice and a heavy-duty tube with careful pressure management is better.

Gearing

Standard on EU EXC models is 14:50. Manual KTM lists rear sprockets from 45 to 52 teeth. For extreme terrain, going to 51 or 52 at the rear shortens first gear and makes the bike easier to control at walking pace. Prefer rear sprocket changes over front: a smaller front sprocket wraps the chain more tightly around the countershaft and accelerates chain and sprocket wear.

After any gearing change: re-check chain tension, confirm the chain guide is not fouling the larger sprocket, and verify the chain has enough adjustment range left.

Suspension

Start from the Comfort column in chapter 9: fork 17 compression and 19 rebound, shock 18 low-speed compression, 2.5 turns high-speed and 18 rebound. Manual Confirm the spring rate matches your weight in full kit first, because clickers cannot compensate for a wrong spring.

Many extreme riders run more sag than the 110 mm rider sag figure to lower the bike and improve rear traction. That reduces bottoming resistance and ground clearance, both of which matter in rocks. Change it deliberately, in small steps, and record what you did.

Controls and ergonomics

Clutch lever

Set for one-finger use

Position the lever so the engagement point falls where your index finger naturally sits when standing. Extreme riding is almost all clutch, and a lever set for seated riding will exhaust your forearm.

Brake lever

Keep the free travel

At least 3 mm free travel on the front lever. Manual Setting it too close risks residual pressure and dragging brakes, which cooks fluid on long descents.

Bar position

Rolled forward for standing

Rotating the bars slightly forward opens the riding position for standing. Re-torque the clamp screws to 20 Nm and the handlebar mount to 40 Nm with Loctite 243 afterwards.

Rear pedal

Slightly low

A rear brake pedal set marginally low is easier to find when standing on the pegs in boots, and less likely to be caught accidentally on a rock.

Surviving repeated crashes

  • Check the subframe fasteners after every event: 35 Nm top with Loctite 243, 30 Nm bottom with Loctite 2701.
  • Check both shock mounting bolts, 80 Nm with Loctite 2701. These loosen more than anything else on a hard-crashed bike.
  • Check spoke tension after every rock section. Six newton metres per nipple.
  • Inspect the radiator fins and mounts after any front-end impact. A cracked radiator ends the day.
  • Re-check the exhaust manifold screw, 15 Nm, and the springs.
  • Look at the chain slider and guide. They wear fastest of all in mud and grit.
Warning · Cooling in extreme terrain

Long low-speed climbs with no airflow are exactly the condition that boils this engine. Confirm the fan works before every event, keep the level 10 mm above the fins, and switch the engine off when stationary rather than idling. If the bike boils over, let it cool fully before opening the cap and check the level again once cold.

Warning · Do not crank a water-filled engine

Water does not compress. Cranking an engine with water in the cylinder can bend the connecting rod. Get the water out first, every time, no exceptions.

Steps

  1. Get the bike out and lay it on its side, exhaust downward, to drain the silencer.
  2. Switch the ignition off.
  3. Remove the spark plug.

  5. Remove the seat and check the airbox and filter. If either is full of water, empty and wring out the filter before continuing.
  6. Check the intake tract is clear of water before restarting.
  7. Dry the plug, or fit the spare, and refit. Aim for 25 Nm; without a wrench, snug plus a small turn.
  8. Start the engine and let it run to clear residual water from the exhaust.
  9. Back at the van: replace the air filter, check the gear oil for milkiness, and check the plug again.

  • Valve cap and nut, easily lost

Steps

  1. Find level ground and something to lean the bike on. Loosen the axle before lifting the wheel.
  2. Remove the wheel following chapter 22.
  3. Deflate fully, remove the valve nut, and loosen the rim lock nuts so the lock can push inward.
  4. Break both beads by standing on the sidewall away from the rim lock.
  5. Lever one side of the tyre off, keeping the opposite bead in the well of the rim. That is what makes it possible.
  6. Remove the old tube and feel carefully all around the inside of the tyre for whatever caused the puncture.
  7. Inflate the new tube just enough to give it shape, and fit it with the valve through the hole.
  8. Work the bead back on by hand as far as possible, then lever the last section, taking care not to pinch the tube.
  9. Inflate, check the bead seats evenly all round, then set the rim lock nuts to 12 Nm and fit the valve nut loosely.
  10. Refit the wheel and torque: 35 Nm front spindle, 80 Nm rear spindle nut. Re-check chain tension on the rear.
Tip · Carry a front tube only

A 21-inch front tube will stretch into an 18-inch rear rim well enough to get you home, so one tube covers both wheels. It halves what you carry. Replace it properly afterwards.

Common mistakes

  • Not pushing the opposite bead into the rim well, which makes the job nearly impossible.
  • Pinching the new tube with a lever, so you do the whole job twice.
  • Failing to find what caused the puncture and immediately puncturing the new tube.
  • Forgetting to retighten the rim lock, so the tyre spins and tears the valve out.
